Advertisement Crops3D: a diverse 3D crop dataset for realistic perception and segmentation toward agricultural applications Download PDF Download PDF Abstract Point cloud analysis is a crucial task in computer vision. Despite significant advances over the past decade, the developments in agricultural domain have faced challenges due to a scarcity of datasets. To facilitate 3D point cloud research in agriculture community, we introduce Crops3D, the diverse real-world dataset derived from authentic agricultural scenarios. Crops3D distinguishes itself through its unique properties: diversity, authenticity, and complexity. The dataset incorporates data from diverse point cloud acquisition methods, encompassing eight distinct crop types with 1,230 samples, authentically representing crops in the real-world. It stands as the pioneering dataset that comprehensively supports the three critical tasks in 3D crop phenotyping: instance segmentation of individual plants in agricultural settings, plant type perception, and plant organ segmentation. Additionally, the intricate crop structures in Crops3D exhibit higher complexity than available 3D public datasets, showcasing substantial self-occlusion and increased complexity as crops mature. We analyse diverse crop point cloud acquisition methods and evaluate multiple models’ performance with the Crops3D dataset. Agriculture Plant sciences Deep Learning Applications in Agricultural Imaging Background & Summary Recent years have witnessed a substantial and noteworthy surge in research on exploring the applications of point clouds within agriculture 1 . This technology has found widespread utilization in various domains including phenotyping research 2 , agricultural robotics 3 and precision agriculture 4 . Point clouds offer invaluable insights, enabling the monitoring of plant status, improving production efficiency, and predicting yields with precision 5 . In the initial phases of agricultural production and crop phenotyping research, gathering information regarding crop growth involved laborious manual observations and measurements, incurring high costs and time investments 6 . The advent of computer vision and image sensors introduced the use of 2D images for parameterized crop representations, significantly enhancing efficiency while reducing costs and enabling more objective assessments 7 . However, despite these advantages, 2D images lack depth information and possess limitations in accurately representing the intricate 3D structures of crops. Conversely, point clouds, as a robust three-dimensional data representation, excel in capturing the complex morphology of crops 8 . In contrast to images, point clouds provide a detailed and intuitive portrayal of the intricate 3D structures of crops 9 . Consequently, their utilization is increasingly pivotal in agricultural contexts. Point cloud data plays a crucial role in high-throughput crop phenotyping, especially when combined with deep learning techniques for automated perception and segmentation. This integration unlocks significant potential by enabling the efficient identification and separation of organs 10 , 11 , 12 . Consequently, point cloud technology facilitates organ-level phenotypic analysis, allowing for precise calculations of 3D biomass, plant height, volume, and leaf area (as illustrated in Table 1 ). This capability allows researchers to rapidly acquire accurate phenotypic information in large-scale agricultural scenarios. Furthermore, when point cloud data encompasses various growth stages of crops, it enables the dynamic tracking of phenotypic parameters across time. By constructing comprehensive growth models, researchers can effectively track crop growth trends, providing multidimensional data support for breeding, cultivation, and phenotype-genotype correlation analysis. Several publicly available point clouds focused on plants have been successively emerged, such as Pheno4D 13 and Rose-X 14 . Pheno4D comprises point clouds from two crop species, namely maize and tomato, with a total of 126 annotated samples. Rose-X contains only 11 rose point clouds. Furthermore, some unpublished datasets, like the one created by Wang et al . 15 , concentrate on specific plants such as lettuce. While these datasets are valuable, they often lack diversity in plant species and sample size, rendering them insufficient for broader research tasks. It is well-recognized the acquisition of large-scale 3D datasets remains a costly and challenging task 16 , particularly within the agricultural sector. Cultivating crops demands ample space, skilled personnel, and lengthy growth cycles, all of which exacerbate the associated costs. Due to these constraints, some point cloud datasets are derived from artificial designs. Analogous to the methodologies employed by ShapeNet 17 and ModelNet40 18 , which utilize CAD generated point clouds, Kaya Turgut et al . utilized L-studio software to generate rose point clouds 19 . Although these generated point clouds resemble real objects, they still exhibit discrepancies that limit their applicability in real-world scenarios. To address the issue of limited dataset availability, we have meticulously curated the Crops3D point cloud dataset over a span of four years, as illustrated in Fig. 1 . This comprehensive dataset encompasses a diverse range of eight crop varieties and over a thousand crop point cloud samples. It stands as the pioneering dataset that comprehensively support the three critical tasks in 3D crop phenotyping: instance segmentation of individual plant in agricultural settings, plant type perception, and plant organ segmentation. This dataset holds the potential to significantly advance research and applications in several domains: (a) Exploration of plant instance segmentation algorithms in complex backgrounds. (b) Development of robust classification algorithms suitable for agricultural scenarios. (c) Research on semantic segmentation algorithms for crop organs and phenotypic parameter extraction. (d) Standardized evaluation dataset for crop point cloud classification and segmentation tasks. Additionally, this paper presents the benchmark results of various widely-used deep learning models on the Crops3D dataset. Methods In this section, the details of the dataset is explored, elaborating on its composition, the methods employed for data acquisition, the procedures involved in processing, the annotations appended. Statistical results are also furnished for a comprehensive understanding. The dataset is primarily designed to facilitate tasks related to crop classification and segmentation. Dataset description The dataset is sourced from extensive agricultural environments, and is specifically designed to capture point clouds that represent a wide variety of plants across large cultivation areas. It integrates data from various point cloud acquisition techniques frequently used in agricultural research, ensuring both comprehensiveness and practical applicability. Data collection was conducted on eight crops: cabbage, cotton, maize, potato, rapeseed, rice, tomato, and wheat. Notably, the data for cabbage and tomatoes were collected periodically, enabling continuous tracking of the growth dynamics. The data for maize spans multiple growth stages, including seedling, tassel, and kernel stages. In contrast, data for cotton, potato, rapeseed, rice, and wheat were focused on short-cycle captures within a specific growth period. Dataset acquisition The dataset is a comprehensive collection, embodying a broad spectrum of acquisition methodologies, each meticulously tailored to cater to the unique requirements of different crops. The methodologies employed span from sensor-based point cloud generation technologies to image-based techniques. In the realm of sensor-based technologies, Terrestrial Laser Scanning (TLS) 20 and Structured Light Scanning 21 are utilized. TLS-based point cloud data collection TLS technology, known for its broad field of view, is exceptionally suited for capturing point clouds in expansive field scenes 22 . As shown in Fig. 2 , a TLS device, the FARO Focus S70 (America) was utilized for point cloud data collection across a variety of crops, including maize, cotton, rice, rapeseed, wheat, and potato. This device provides a maximum resolution of 976,000 points per second, enabling rapid and precise data acquisition. The accuracy of TLS results is influenced by factors such as site layout and equipment settings. Important considerations include the distance and angle between the scanner and the target. Excessive distance reduces point cloud density, while insufficient distance might result in data loss. Each plant within the field point cloud data was segmented individually to create a dataset of single-plant point clouds. Fig. 2 illustrates the acquisition scenarios for six crop species. Fig. 2 Full size image TLS-based point cloud generation. Image-based point cloud data generation: tomato In addition to utilizing TLS for field point cloud data acquisition, image-based methods are commonly employed to generate individual plant point clouds. Specifically, tomato images were collected in a greenhouse located in LiaoCheng, Shandong, China from August to October 2020. During the seedling stage, around 100 photos were taken of each tomato plant. As the plants continued to grow, they were divided into upper and lower sections for separate image collection due to occlusion. The Structure from Motion (SfM) 23 algorithm was then used to reconstruct the tomatoes in 3D, resulting in a complete 3D point cloud, as shown in Fig. 3 . For detailed technical information, please refer to the referenced publication, 3DPhenoMVS 16 . The dataset comprises 83 tomato point cloud samples, encompassing various growth stages of the tomato plants. Fig. 3 Full size image Image-based point cloud generation for tomato plants. Cabbage point cloud generation using both technologies In this study, laser scanning and image-based methods were employed to generate point cloud data for purple cabbage plants. The active data collection involved the use of a structured light scanner, while the image-based approach utilized an enhanced CasMVSNet, a deep learning-based network, for 3D reconstruction. Data from 14 purple cabbage plants in a natural setting were collected weekly from September 1 to December 5, 2022, using both RGB images and structured light data. Images were captured with a Canon 77D camera at a resolution of 6,000 × 4,000 pixels, while a Wiiboox Reeyee-Pro scanner provided 3D point cloud data. Fig. 4 illustrates the acquisition methods, showing RGB data collected in 360 degrees at varying heights. During the seedling stage, 60–80 images were taken at two heights, increasing to 100–120 as plants grew. An improved CasMVSNet was employed to generate 3D structures from these images 24 , while the scanner synthesized structured light data to generate point clouds. Fig. 4 Full size image Purple cabbage data collection. Comparison of acquisition methods It is noteworthy that each point cloud acquisition method possesses specific advantages and limitations. TLS is particularly suited for large agricultural crops and extensive environments due to its exceptional range and precision. However, its high cost presents a significant drawback. Structured light scanning, on the other hand, is highly effective in obtaining high-quality crop point clouds but is limited by a narrow field of view and sensitivity to lighting conditions. Therefore, it is more suitable for smaller crops and controlled indoor environments. Image-based point cloud generation is renowned for its cost-effectiveness, but it necessitates complex data processing algorithms, such as the Multi-View Stereo (MVS) deep learning approach CasMVSNet 25 . Acquiring complete and accurate point cloud data is essential for the precise extraction of phenotypic parameters. Missing significant portions of point cloud data can introduce errors in identification and segmentation, thereby complicating subsequent phenotypic analyses. Therefore, it is crucial to consider the morphological and structural characteristics of the crops when selecting an appropriate acquisition method. For example, crops with elongated leaves such as rice and wheat lack distinct features in images, rendering image-based methods less effective. In such cases, sensor-based approaches like TLS can yield superior point cloud data. Conversely, for crops with broad leaves and rich features, such as cabbage, TLS, structured light, and image-based methods are all viable options. Notably, mutual occlusion and self-occlusion remain pervasive challenges that none of these methods can fully address. Data annotation To facilitate the use of this dataset by researchers, the organs of all crop samples were annotated in this study. CloudCompare (version 2.12.4) software was employed to annotate the point cloud. This software is a powerful open-source point cloud processing tool that provides various point cloud processing operators, such as filtering and denoising. It also supports various editing and cropping operations, providing convenience for point cloud annotation processing. When performing semantic annotation of plant point clouds, common practices include using different colors to mark or adding scalar field (SF) attributes to the point cloud. However, modifying the point cloud color may overwrite the original color information, so in most cases, adding a new attribute column to the point cloud data has become a more commonly used semantic annotation method. Using the maize point cloud as a case study, statistical filtering was applied to the individual plant point cloud to remove any outliers. CloudCompare was then utilized to segregate the point cloud into six distinct semantic segments: soil, pot container, stem, leaves, tassel, and fruit. Each segment was allocated a unique scalar field (SF) attribute value. Following this segmentation, these annotated point clouds were combined to create a comprehensive annotated point cloud dataset, as illustrated in Fig. 5 . Within the plot point clouds, the data was primarily divided into ground and plant sections. The SF for the ground section was labeled as 0, while the plant sections were labeled from 1 to n to distinguish different plant objects, where n represents the total number of distinct maize plants in the plot. Various parts of the crop point cloud were then annotated for detailed analysis. Currently, many point cloud deep learning models necessitate a consistent number of points across the point clouds. However, the natural variability in plant sizes leads to differing point counts, posing a significant challenge. To address this, it’s essential to subsample the point clouds to ensure a uniform number of points across the dataset. Farthest Point Sampling (FPS) is a widely used method for sampling point clouds, and it allows for uniform sampling across the point cloud while preserving geometric details. Considering the structural diversity in plant stems and leaves, conventional sampling points such as 2,048 or 4,096 may result in the loss of significant details. To retain more crop features, FPS was utilized to sample the original point cloud to 10,000 points, thereby constructing the Crops3D_10k dataset. Additionally, instance segmentation annotations were conducted on plant cultivation plots obtained via TLS, resulting in the Crops3D_IS dataset. Corrupt dataset In light of the unique challenges of data collection in agricultural environments, where noise and missing data are prevalent, the ability of models to accurately perceive crops is of significant importance in agricultural practice. To comprehensively test the performance of model classification, we construct a corruption dataset named Crops3D_10k-C, derived from Crops3D_10k using methods from modlenet-C 26 . As illustrated in Fig. 6 , this dataset comprises seven types of corrupted point clouds. This dataset specifically tests the robustness of classification models against various abnormal data challenges. Therefore, we provide four subdivisions of the dataset: Crops3D, subsampled Crops3D_10k, Crops3D_10k-C for evaluating classification robustness, and Crops3D_IS for instance segmentation. Fig. 6 Full size image Clean point clouds and corrupt point clouds constructed with seven different corruptions. The Crops3D_10k-C dataset is comprised of seven types of corruptions, designed to simulate various real-world phenomena that may affect point cloud data. These corruptions include: Jitter Implemented by applying small, random displacements to the positions of points within the point cloud, this corruption simulates the effect of sensor jitter or random errors during data collection. The displacements follow a Gaussian distribution with mean \(\mu =0\) and standard deviation \({\rm{\sigma }}=10\times {\rm{level}}\) . Rotation and scale These transformations simulate the effects of rotational movements due to changes in object or sensor positioning, and dimensional changes due to crop growth, respectively. Rotation is performed randomly around the XYZ axes, with the degree of rotation increasing with the level, up to a maximum of π /6. Scaling involves random dimensional adjustments in the xyz axes, controlled by a scaling factor s. Global point addition (Add G ) and local point addition (Add L ) These methods involve adding points either globally across the entire point cloud or locally within specific regions. This simulates sensor noise or anomalies during data acquisition by randomly interspersing additional points. \({\rm{Ad}}{{\rm{d}}}_{{\rm{G}}}\) generates \(10\times {\rm{level}}\) new points within a unit sphere centered at the centroid of the point cloud, integrating these points with the original point cloud. \({\rm{Ad}}{{\rm{d}}}_{{\rm{L}}}\) randomly selects a seed point from the point cloud and generating \(10\times {\rm{level}}\) new points around it. These points follow a Gaussian distribution, with the standard deviation ( σ ) being randomly chosen from a uniform distribution U (0.075,0.125). Drop global point (Drop G ) and drop local point (Drop L ) These techniques model data loss by randomly removing points across the entire point cloud or within specified local areas. For Drop G , a percentage of points defined by the severity level is removed, whereas for Drop L , the \(100\times {\rm{level}}\) nearest points to a selected seed point are removed. Each of the seven corruptions is applied at five different severity levels, providing a graded approach to studying the impact of these perturbations on subsequent point cloud processing tasks. The data is stored and compressed into a file named Crops3D.zip, which includes four directories: Crops3D, Crops3D_10k, Crops3D_100k-C, and Crops3D_IS. Crops3D directory The Crops3D directory contains annotated raw point cloud data. It includes eight directories and two text files. The eight directories are named Cabbage, Cotton, Maize, Potato, Rapeseed, Rice, Tomato, and Wheat, each storing PLY point cloud files corresponding to the respective crop. The two text files list the relative paths of samples in the training and test sets. Crops3D_10k directory The Crops3D_10k directory contains point cloud data subsampled to 10,000 points using FPS. The file structure mirrors that of Crops3D, ensuring consistency and ease of use. Crops3D_10k-C directory The Crops3D_IS directory contains a corrupt directory and two HDF5 (H5) files representing the training and test datasets. The corrupt directory contains 36 H5 files: one clean test set and 35 files corresponding to seven types of corrupted test sets, each divided into five severity levels. Crops3D_IS directory The Crops3D_IS directory contains point cloud data intended for individual plant segmentation at the plot scale, known as instance segmentation. It contains one subdirectory and two text files. This subdirectory stores point cloud data for 50 plots, covering maize, potato, and rapeseed crops, formatted in PLY. The two text files enumerate the relative paths of samples in the training and test sets. Technical Validation The annotation quality of the dataset was validated by evaluating the consistency between the labeled data and annotations from multiple experts (E1, E2, E3, E4, E5, E6, E7). For comparison, sixteen point cloud samples were randomly selected from the dataset and provided to seven experts for manual segmentation. Subsequently, the mean Intersection over Union (mIoU) and Accuracy (Acc) between the dataset ground truth (GT) and the different expert annotations were calculated. Additionally, the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) and Cohen’s Kappa were used to indicate the level of agreement between the GT and expert annotations. Fig. 7(a,b) illustrate the mIoU and Acc of the dataset’s GT compared to annotations by various experts. The results demonstrate a strong consistency between the dataset GT and the expert annotations, with overall mIoU exceeding 0.91 and Acc surpassing 0.97, indicating high annotation accuracy. Fig. 7(c,d ) present the ICC and Kappa values between the dataset GT and the seven expert annotations. For the sixteen samples analyzed, the overall ICC exceeded 0.98, and Kappa exceeded 0.97. The lowest ICC for an individual sample was 0.85, and the lowest Kappa was 0.80, further confirming a high level of consistency between the dataset GT and the expert annotations. This evaluation measures their performance on crop point clouds, covering both classification and segmentation tasks using the Crops3D dataset. Compared to publicly available datasets for other 3D objects, Crops3D presents unique characteristics, including a diverse range of crop species and an extended growth coverage. These distinctive qualities set Crops3D dataset apart, offering a challenging and expansive repository for conducting point cloud classification and segmentation tasks within the realm of agricultural applications. This comprehensive evaluation will provide valuable insights for point cloud processing in agriculture, assisting researchers and practitioners in making informed decisions when selecting suitable models. Robust perception Evaluating indicator Object-level point cloud classification is one of the fundamental tasks in point cloud perception, with the robustness of classification primarily depending on architecture design, pre-training and data augmentation methods. In this section, we evaluate the performance of multiple models on the classification task using the Crops3D dataset. We primarily leverage the corruption dataset, Crops3D_10k-C, introduced in Methods Section, to assess the classification robustness of various models on crop corruption data without pre-training and data augmentation. The specific formula for computing mean Corruption Error (mCE) is as follows (Eq. 2 ), primarily using the performance metrics of DGCNN 28 as the baseline to normalize the error rates for various corruption types (Eq. 1 ). $${{\rm{CE}}}_{i}=\frac{\mathop{\sum }\limits_{l=1}^{5}\left(1-{{\rm{OA}}}_{i,l}\right)}{\mathop{\sum }\limits_{l=1}^{5}\left(1-{{\rm{OA}}}_{i,l}^{{\rm{DGCNN}}}\right)}$$ (1) $$\text{mCE}\,=\,\frac{1}{{\rm{N}}}\mathop{\sum }\limits_{i=1}^{{\rm{N}}}{\text{CE}}_{i}$$ (2) Here, i represents various types of corruptions, including adding points, removing points, jittering, rotating, and scaling. l , ranging from small to large, denotes the severity of the perturbation. \({{\rm{OA}}}_{i,l}\) denotes the Overall Accuracy of the model on the i -th type of corruption at severity level l , and \({{\rm{OA}}}_{i,l}^{{\rm{DGCNN}}}\) indicates the Overall Accuracy of the baseline model DGCNN for the same corruption type and level. N represents the number of corrupt settings. A lower mCE signifies robustness of the model to corruption data, suggesting that the model is better equipped to handle variations and maintain accuracy across corrupted datasets. This evaluation method provides insights into each model’s generalization capability in realistic scenarios where data corruption is prevalent. Full size table Segmentation Point cloud segmentation is a fundamental task in the field of computer vision, encompassing both semantic and instance segmentation. Semantic segmentation aiming to partition point cloud data into subsets with semantic information, while instance segmentation focuses on distinguishing distinct objects or instances within the same category. In the agricultural domain, particularly in the processing of crop point clouds, this task becomes especially vital as it provides foundational support for numerous modern agricultural applications. To gain a deeper understanding of the feasibility of point cloud segmentation in the agricultural domain, nine widely used point cloud segmentation models were selected: PointNet, PointNet++, DGCNN, PointCNN, PointMLP, CurveNet, Stratified Transformer (ST) 37 , PointMamba, and DeLA. For instance segmentation, the current state-of-the-art models on the S3DIS dataset, Mask3D 38 and OneFormer3D 39 , were chosen. Detailed testing and evaluation were conducted on the Crops3D dataset. The aim was to evaluate the usability and investigate segmentation models that are better suited for crop point cloud segmentation. Evaluating indicator To comprehensively assess the performance of these models in crop point cloud organ segmentation tasks, this study employs two common semantic segmentation evaluation metrics: Acc and Intersection over Union (IoU). Acc provides an intuitive measure reflecting the model’s ability to correctly segment individual points, while IoU measures the degree of overlap between the model’s segmentation results and the ground truth labels, providing a more robust measure of segmentation accuracy. For evaluating instance segmentation, the Average Precision (AP) metric is used. AP measures the precision of the model’s ability to accurately identify and delineate individual instances across varying levels of detection confidence. The specific formulas are as follows: $${\rm{Acc}}=\frac{{\rm{TP}}+{\rm{TN}}}{{\rm{TP}}+{\rm{TN}}+{\rm{FP}}+{\rm{FN}}}$$ (3) $${\rm{IoU}}=\frac{{\rm{TP}}}{{\rm{TP}}+{\rm{FP}}+{\rm{FN}}}$$ (4) $${\rm{AP}}={\int }_{0}^{1}p(r){dr}$$ (5) In this context, True Positives (TP), True Negatives (TN), False Positives (FP), and False Negatives (FN) are the four fundamental elements constituting the confusion matrix in machine learning, which are used to evaluate the performance of models. In calculating AP, two key metrics are precision ( p ) and recall ( r ). By averaging the Acc, IoU and AP of each organ category, mAcc, mIoU, and mAP can be obtained. These metrics reflect the comprehensive segmentation performance at the scale of the entire crop. The specific formulas are as follows: $${\rm{mAcc}}=\frac{1}{N}{\sum }_{i=1}^{N}{{\rm{Acc}}}_{i}$$ (6) $${\rm{mIoU}}=\frac{1}{N}{\sum }_{i=1}^{N}{{\rm{IoU}}}_{i}$$ (7) $${\rm{mAP}}=\frac{1}{N}{\sum }_{i=1}^{N}{{\rm{AP}}}_{i}$$ (8) In this context, N represents the total number of categories in the plant point cloud, where i denotes the i -th organ category (1 ≤ i ≤ N).
